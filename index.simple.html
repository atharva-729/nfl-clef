The aim

Can a model watch an NFL broadcast and tell us what is happening?

Short answer: yesfor the scorebug and the scoring story of the game
Short answer: unprovenfor judgement calls like highlights and ad breaks
  • QuestionGiven only the picture, can a model follow a football game well enough to drive auto-clipping, live app updates or archive tagging?
  • TestThe whole 4th quarter of Jets at Bengals, Week 8, 2025: 34 minutes, one frame every 5 seconds.
  • MethodCloudflare's Clef answers the same 24 multiple-choice questions on every frame, with a confidence for each answer.
  • CheckAnswers scored against labels built from the official play-by-play. When an answer changes and holds, we call it a game event.

04 How it was done

Sample, ask, check, detect, replay.

Sample408 stills, one every 5 s, covering the 4th quarter.
Ask24 fixed multiple-choice questions, plus a note on where the scorebug is and what to ignore.
CheckScorebug labels read from every frame and cross-checked against the official play-by-play. Other labels drafted by AI.
DetectAn answer at 60% confidence or more, held for two frames, becomes an event.
ReplayRecorded once, so this page only replays files and spends no tokens.
Caveats

We made it easy.

Clef was told exactly where the scorebug is and what each part means, and got a zoomed crop. Another network's graphics need a new description.

One quarter, one game.

One broadcast, labels read by one AI reader and cross-checked against the play-by-play. Treat the numbers as an indication, not a benchmark.

Judgement labels are soft.

Replay, play phase, highlight-worthy, ad-break safe and sponsor labels are one reader's call, only spot-checked. They are kept out of the headline figure.
